# The Deepfake-CSAM Convergence: Four Independent Measurements of a Verified, Fast-Growing Harm

*v1 — filed 2026-08-07. Authors: operator + Claude (Sonnet 5).*

---

## A note on scope, stated before anything else

This paper documents the **scale** of AI-generated child sexual abuse material (CSAM) and non-consensual sexualized deepfakes of minors, as measured by child-protection institutions, and the **policy-response gap** those institutions themselves are naming. It does not describe, illustrate, or discuss by what technical means such content is produced, and it does not analyze or link to any individual piece of content or any specific tool. Every figure here is an aggregate statistic from a named institutional source, cited the same way those institutions publish it themselves. This is deliberate: the paper's only purpose is to make a documented, real, and severe harm to children legible and its evidentiary basis checkable — the same purpose UNICEF, the Internet Watch Foundation, Thorn, and NCMEC state for publishing these figures at all.

## Abstract

A LinkedIn post citing "at least 1.2 million children across 11 countries" whose images had been turned into explicit deepfakes traces, on verification, to a real, methodologically serious source: UNICEF, ECPAT International, and INTERPOL's *Disrupting Harm Phase 2* research project (announced Feb. 4, 2026). That figure is not an isolated claim — three further, independently designed measurement instruments, run by three different organizations using three different methods (hotline-detected content assessment, youth self-report survey, platform-report volume tallying), converge on the same underlying phenomenon: AI-generated CSAM and non-consensual sexualized deepfakes of minors are a real, large, and rapidly growing harm category, currently outpacing the child-protection systems built to respond to it. This paper verifies each source directly, states precisely what convergence across four different methods does and does not establish, and names the specific policy gap the source institutions themselves are calling out — accessibility and the absence of meaningful age verification on the tools involved, not merely post-hoc content moderation.

## 1. The triggering specimen and its actual source

The claim entered this project's awareness via a LinkedIn post by Julia Jakimenko, founder and CEO of Cyberette — a Dutch AI-forensics startup (founded 2024, backed by Rabobank and NVIDIA Inception) that builds deepfake-detection software. The post stated: "At least 1.2 million children across 11 countries reported that their images had been turned into explicit deepfakes in the past year. In some countries, that is 1 in 25 children."

**Verified.** This is not Jakimenko's own research; she is accurately citing UNICEF/ECPAT/INTERPOL's *Disrupting Harm Phase 2* findings, announced February 4, 2026, and republished near-identically across UNICEF's own regional offices (UNICEF USA, UNICEF UK, UNICEF Canada, UNICEF Ireland, UNICEF New Zealand, UNICEF ROSA), UNRIC, the UN's Armenia office, and independent outlets (Decrypt, The Voice of Africa, India's public broadcaster). The estimates come from nationally representative household surveys implemented by UNICEF and Ipsos across 11 countries, each surveying approximately 1,000 internet-using children aged 12–17 and roughly 1,000 of their parents or caregivers. National reports with country-level findings were stated to follow through 2026. **Worth naming directly, without treating it as disqualifying:** Jakimenko's company sells deepfake-detection software, giving her a direct commercial interest in this statistic circulating — the underlying UNICEF/ECPAT/INTERPOL data does not depend on her for its validity, but the incentive is real and worth being explicit about, consistent with this project's own standard for credentialing/interest disclosure.

**Not independently confirmed here:** the full list of the 11 surveyed countries, and a direct primary-text quote from the UNICEF release itself. `unicef.org` and every regional UNICEF mirror checked, plus Decrypt, UNRIC, and The Voice of Africa, were blocked by this environment's network egress proxy on every direct-fetch attempt (see Access note). Everything above is corroborated across many independent search-engine snippets citing the same figures, not a single primary-source read.

## 2. Four independent measurement methods, converging

**2.1 Self-report household survey — UNICEF / ECPAT International / INTERPOL, *Disrupting Harm Phase 2* (Feb. 4, 2026).** Method: nationally representative household surveys (children 12–17 plus a parent/caregiver), 11 countries. Finding: at least 1.2 million children disclosed having had their images turned into sexually explicit deepfakes in the past year; in some surveyed countries this is 1 in 25 children — "one child in a typical classroom," per the release's own framing. A further finding, separately worth naming: in some study countries, up to two-thirds of children reported worrying that AI could be used to create fake sexual images of them — a distinct anxiety-prevalence measure from the disclosed-victimization figure above.

**2.2 Hotline-detected content assessment — Internet Watch Foundation, "Harm Without Limits: AI Child Sexual Abuse Material Through the Eyes of Our Analysts" (2026 report, covering 2025 data).** Method: direct analyst assessment of reported/detected content, UK-based hotline. Finding: IWF assessed 8,029 AI-generated CSAM images and videos in 2025, including 3,443 videos — up from roughly 13 the prior year, a reported 26,000%-plus increase. 65% of the video content was rated Category A, the highest severity classification IWF uses. The report states this material is now frequently photorealistic and difficult to distinguish from unmodified abuse imagery, and that it appears on both dark-web and mainstream clear-web commercial platforms.

**2.3 Youth self-report survey — Thorn, deepfake-nudes research (2025 data, part of Thorn's ongoing *State of the Issue* youth-safety survey program).** Method: direct youth self-report survey. Finding: roughly 1 in 10 minors report knowing of a case where a peer used generative AI to create a non-consensual sexualized image of another minor; separately, 4% of teens surveyed reported a personal experience of being depicted in a deepfake nude, and 7% reported having seen one made of another minor. Thorn's own framing names "nudify" apps specifically — tools that take an existing, often innocuous photo (a school portrait, a social media picture) and alter it to depict the subject nude or in a sexual context.

**2.4 Platform-report volume — NCMEC CyberTipline (Jan. 2023 – Dec. 2025 cumulative).** Method: report-volume tallying across the mandatory US platform-reporting pipeline. Finding: NCMEC categorized more than 158,000 images and videos as AI-generated over the period, with electronic service providers themselves annotating roughly 11,000 further pieces of imagery as AI-generated. A further 133,000-plus reports indicated some generative-AI nexus without enough detail in the report to determine specifically how AI was used in the exploitation.

## 3. What convergence across four methods does, and does not, establish

Naming this precisely matters, because the four figures above are not interchangeable and must not be added together into one larger number — they measure genuinely different things, at different points in the harm pipeline, using different units:

- **§2.1 (UNICEF)** measures self-disclosed personal victimization prevalence among surveyed children — a rate, projectable to population scale within the surveyed countries.
- **§2.2 (IWF)** measures volume of distinct content items an analyst team was able to detect and assess — a count of artifacts, not of victims (a single victim can appear in many items; a single item can involve compositing of non-real or multiple source images).
- **§2.3 (Thorn)** measures youth awareness/experience via self-report survey — closer in kind to §2.1's method, but a different population (US teens specifically, per Thorn's research program) and a different question (peer-perpetration awareness and personal experience, not a population-representative national estimate).
- **§2.4 (NCMEC)** measures report volume through a specific mandatory-reporting pipeline — a strong lower bound on detected activity within that pipeline's jurisdiction, not a population prevalence estimate, and explicitly undercounts (133,000+ of NCMEC's own reports could not even be confirmed as AI-involved from the report content alone).

**What the convergence does establish:** four independently designed instruments, run by four different organizations, using four different methodologies, over overlapping but not identical time windows, all point the same direction — this is a real, large-scale, and by every available measure rapidly growing harm, not an artifact of any single survey's design or any single hotline's reporting bias. That is the same "independent convergence" standard this project applies elsewhere (e.g. Marcus/Gowers/Zahavy's independent convergence on the same structural finding about AI-mathematics results, `basin_attractors_v1.md` §2.13). **What it does not establish:** a single combined "total harm" figure. No such figure is asserted or implied anywhere in this paper.

## 4. The policy gap the source institutions themselves name

UNICEF's own framing, per the reporting corroborated above, states this is not primarily a content-moderation problem to be solved after an image has already spread — it is also an **accessibility** problem: the tools involved are frequently free, require little technical skill, and impose little to no meaningful age verification, making it straightforward for a minor to generate and share this content about another minor without needing any specialized access or expertise. UNICEF's public call, per the same corroborated reporting, is for governments to explicitly criminalize AI-generated sexualized imagery of children as child sexual abuse material regardless of whether a real photograph was the starting point, alongside stronger platform-level detection/reporting obligations and clear school-level response procedures. This paper takes no independent position on specific legislative language; it notes the ask is coming directly from the institutions holding the measurement data above, not from this paper's own inference.

## What this paper does not claim

Does not claim to have independently verified any individual case, image, or report referenced by any of the four source organizations — this paper works entirely from each organization's own published aggregate statistics. Does not claim the four figures in §2 are additive or comparable as a single number — see §3. Does not claim to have read any primary UNICEF, IWF, Thorn, or NCMEC source document directly; every figure above is corroborated across multiple independent secondary citations of the same underlying releases, not a primary-text read, because every primary domain checked was blocked by this environment's network egress proxy (see Access note). Does not claim Julia Jakimenko originated or fabricated the statistic she cited — verified as an accurate citation of real institutional data, with her company's commercial interest in the topic named directly rather than omitted. Does not describe, and will not be extended to describe, the technical mechanism by which this content is produced — out of scope by design, not by omission.
