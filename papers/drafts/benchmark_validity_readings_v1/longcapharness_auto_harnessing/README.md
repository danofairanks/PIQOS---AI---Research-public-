# What the Average Counts: Reading LongCapHarness, a Harness Built by a Stronger Agent Against Labels It Can Read

*AI-assisted. Living research: staged, not peer reviewed, may be revised or withdrawn.*

**Scope of naming.** One paper, "LongCapHarness: Capability-Guided Auto-Harnessing for Long-Context Reasoning" (arXiv:2611.10463v1, 7 Nov 2026, 12 pp.; the author block names Microsoft Research Asia). The authors are called "the authors"; works the paper cites are described by role. The developer model is a model from another developer (named in the paper as GPT-5.6-Sol); the execution models are Llama3-8B and Qwen2.5-3B. No intent is attributed to anyone.

**Disclosure.** AI-assisted (Claude, working under this project's own non-default standard; direction, selection and release decisions are the operator's); single reader. No model from the assisting model's developer appears in the text read. Read from an operator-supplied copy (PDF sha256 beginning 990f1afa94964c7c; not stored here): all 12 pages as images; no code, library or development logs were available (none is mentioned in the text).

## What it is
A "capability harness" library built once per task type. A developer agent studies development records with reference answers, decomposes a task into capability primitives (code or model calls), control logic and capability state, and revises them in a loop it labels recursive self-improvement until the harness's answer is close to the reference, "the stop condition is decided by the harnessing agent". At test time a small model selects a harness by its text description and runs it; code primitives operate on the stored long text outside the model's context. Evaluated on HELMET, RULER and LongBench-v2 (2,242 development and 59,826 held-out test records; development and test records are disjoint). Headline: the highest aggregate score among the compared methods on both execution models, above data-synthesis plus post-training baselines and an earlier recursive-self-improvement baseline, with an estimated development cost "less than 50% of the estimated training costs" of two of them.

## Flags (ranked; post hoc; leads, not verdicts)
1. **The gain is concentrated in tasks a program can solve, and trails on the semantic categories.** Recall reaches 100.00 with both models (keyword matching, lookup) and RULER 94.62 and 94.28 (relation following, state tracking, counting). On the categories the paper itself calls semantic the harness is at or below the best compared method: RAG -4.93 (Llama, against the earlier RSI baseline) and -0.55 (Qwen), LongQA -0.46 and -1.10. HELMET alone gives margins of +5.21 (Llama, against NExtLong) and +11.65 (Qwen, against PolicyLong), against +13.12 and +17.61 on RULER. The paper says so in one sentence of its own ("Harnesses organize these operations but do not replace their semantic judgements."); the title and abstract say "long-context reasoning". (E1)
2. **The aggregate weights RULER at half.** "Avg" is the unweighted mean of the HELMET average and RULER, verified for every row; RULER is 54% of the test records and the part where code does the work, so the choice of aggregate sets how large the margin looks. (E1)
3. **Two of HELMET's seven categories are missing from the main table.** Citation and Summarization are in the split (970 and 700 test records, plus development records) and have no column in Table 1; the HELMET average is the mean of the other five, verified for all six rows. The reason is not stated. (E1)
4. **A program-plus-model system is compared with models, and the baselines are not like-for-like.** The base models are run at their short native windows (8K for Llama) on inputs up to 128K (Llama zero-shot 15.14); no simple pipeline baseline (retrieval or chunking with the same model, or plain code) is reported. The paper's own Table 3 shows the reranking starting point, BM25 plus sort with no model in the loop, at a mean of 40.35, above the final Llama harness (39.67) and above the NExtLong Llama reranking score in Table 1 (31.47). Several baseline cells (EntropyLong, PolicyLong, parts of NExtLong for Qwen) are taken from those papers; how evaluation protocols were matched is not stated. (E1; E2)
5. **A reranking evaluation set appears to overlap the development set, or two tables describe the same numbers differently.** Table 1's harness reranking scores (39.67 and 56.28) equal the means of the final version in Table 3, which the text says is evaluated on "the same 500 records, with 100 at each input length"; Table 6 lists 405 re-rank test and 210 development records (615 in total), so at least 95 of those 500 cannot be test records. Either the main-table score includes development records or the description in Section 4.2.3 is wrong. (E1 for the counts and the equality; E3 for which is true)
6. **The loop improves Qwen's reranking and not Llama's.** Section 4.2.3 reports that the primitive's evolution "improves task performance"; for Llama the final version (39.67) and the intermediate one (38.95) are both below the initial non-model baseline (40.35), while Qwen goes from 40.35 to 56.28. Llama is the model the developer loop executed candidates on. (E1)
7. **The "self-improvement" is a stronger external agent iterating against labels it can read, with a stop rule it sets.** The developer agent compares the harness's answers to the reference answers, writes the code that produces the scored answers, and decides when the difference is small enough. That is the pattern the gate-audit and TRUST readings in this repository describe: the editor reads the gate's inputs and shares the loop's authority. With templated tasks (34 subtasks; as few as 5 development records for some RULER subtasks) code can encode the template. No defeat condition or check beyond a disjoint split is described; the paper does note that weaker models as harnessing agents fail. (E2)
8. **The cost comparison joins unlike things, and one figure does not reproduce from the stated rates.** The development cost ($541.47 in API charges) is 29% of the estimate for PolicyLong's training and 15% of NExtLong's, which are GPU rental for general-purpose models; the harness is built per task family and uses more input at inference (Llama 408M against 236M tokens for the earlier RSI baseline; Qwen $53.74 against $29.39); the training baselines' inference cost is not counted; development used 15.5 times the earlier baseline's input tokens (814M against 52M). Development plus inference is $549.70 (Llama) and $595.21 (Qwen) against $339.86 and $360.95 for the earlier baseline. That baseline's development cost, $331.56, is above the maximum the paper's stated rates allow for 52.42M input and 3.87M output tokens ($287.08 with every input token at the ordinary rate); a pricing component is missing from the description. (E1)
9. **LongBench-v2 is Qwen only and not matched.** The five non-code domains (453 questions, 50 used for development, 403 scored) against published baseline numbers; Medium 34.47 against 27.10 (+7.37), Long 27.85 against 31.50 (-3.65). Per-length counts are not given; on a few hundred questions a few points is about one standard error (about 4 points at n = 100 to 150, illustrative). The text reports the win and the loss accurately; no intervals. (E1; E3)
10. **No intervals, seeds or repeated builds; no release statement.** One harness library per setting from one development run; no variance across development runs; the cross-model result (79.32 to 78.73) is one pair. (E2)

## What holds
The margins in the text recompute from Table 1; the aggregate and the HELMET average are exactly what the rows imply; the training-cost estimates and all four inference costs recompute at the stated rates; the limitation paragraph names the dependence on a strong harnessing agent; the Q1 section says plainly where the harness does not help. Reporting a per-input-length plot and the harness's intermediate versions (Table 3) is what allowed flags 4 to 6 to be checked at all.
