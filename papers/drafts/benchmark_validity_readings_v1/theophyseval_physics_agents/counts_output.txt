We evaluated agents on two tracks, which we term the “isolated track” and the “realistic track.” These are dis- tinguished by whether or not the agent is allowed to ac- cess the internet. In our experiments we found that, in the realistic track, GPT-6-Astra with Codex (Astra) achieved a near-perfect accuracy of 98%, and both Astra and Opus 5.5 with Claude Code CLI (Opus) achieved an accuracy of 92% in the isolated track. The average change in accuracy for most agents between the two tracks was quite small. Since many of our problems rely on formulas from the literature, this indicates that leading LLMs have effec- tively memorized large parts of the theoretical physics literature. Our results indicate that some frontier models may have already surpassed the best human researchers in their ability to perform concrete calculations, even in spe- cialized topics. This is a significant milestone, with impli- cations for education and research in theoretical physics. The ability to solve concrete problems has traditionally been highly valued in theoretical physics. Problem solv- ing is central to physics education [12, 13]. Students are evaluated through high school competitions such as the International Physics Olympiad [14], examinations used in graduate admissions [15], and graduate qualifying ex- ams [16]. However, problem-solving abilities are only one of the skills that theoretical physicists require. The main aim of the field is to construct theories of the physical world. Second, it is necessary to ask the “right” high-level ques- tions of the theory. Third, it is necessary to convert these questions into concrete calculations. Therefore, it is only at a late stage in the scientific process that one is faced with a concrete calculation with a verifiable answer. As a corollary, the excellence of AI agents at prob- lem solving does not immediately imply their all-round excellence at autonomous physics research. Human ex- perts are required in the loop. Autonomous excellence at research might emerge in the near future but will require more than problem-solving ability. We hope that the assessment presented in this paper will aid discussions in the theoretical physics community on the evolution of pedagogical and research practices. Similar discussions are underway in the mathematical community [17, 18]. It is desirable for the theoretical physics community to have these discussions in advance, before AI has a significantly disruptive impact. Several agents included in this study do not yet sat- urate our evaluation. For example, among open-weight models, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (DeepSeek) harnessed in Codex scored 54% and 50% on the realistic and isolated tracks, respectively. We hope that our collection of prob- lems will be useful for model developers. It might serve as a method of measuring the abilities of agents to assist theoretical physicists in their research. A. Previous work There has been considerable previous work on bench- marking the abilities of LLMs on school [19, 20], compe- tition [21, 22], and undergraduate physics [23]. Bench- marks such as TPBench [24], SciCode [25], Frontier- Science [26], PRL-Bench [27], QMBench [28] and notably CritPt [29] include research-level problems in addition to simpler problems. Our paper adds to this literature but is distinguished by its exclusive focus on modern research- level problems. Our evaluation methods are also different since most other evaluations do not usually allow agents to access the full range of tools that they would have access to in the real world. Terminal-Bench-Science [30] is broader in its cover- age than this assessment, which is focused on theoret- ical physics and provides deeper coverage within that field. Moreover, since we seek to evaluate agents on re- alistic problems that physicists would encounter, we do not discard problems simply because they can be solved by agents. Our evaluation is roughly comparable to the “First Proof” challenge in mathematics [31–34] in the sense that it involves unpublished research-level problems. How- ever, while First Proof involves human assessment of proofs, our evaluation is automated and only based on the final answers. This paper is organized as follows. In Section II we explain the class of problems included in our collection. In Section III we describe the procedure used to evaluate agents on this collection. In Section IV, we provide a summary of the quantitative and qualitative conclusions of our evaluation. We conclude with some discussion in Section V. The appendices provide details. Problems The problems in our collection were obtained by so- liciting world experts from different areas of theoretical physics. We asked experts to contribute problems that met the following three criteria. 1. Problems related to current physics research; 2. A known solution that was simple to verify but dif- ficult to guess; 3. A solution that could not be looked up in the pub- lished literature or elsewhere. We also requested that contributors not publish their so- lutions in the near future, to avoid contamination of the evaluation. These criteria are quite restrictive. In particular, they exclude problems from ongoing research that the experts intended to publish in the near future. One pathway for contributors to satisfy these criteria was to give us a problem from their research notes that they had worked out but not taken the trouble to publish. A more common alternative was for contributors to adapt a problem solved in one of their recent papers. By changing some parameters in a physics calculation, it is often possible to obtain a new problem with an answer that cannot be guessed from the old answer. Problem contributors could solve such a problem by modifying their existing code or calculations. But another expert human, or an AI agent, would need to locate and under- stand the original solution; reconstruct the calculation in detail; and then adapt it to the modified problem. Such an exercise is often assigned to graduate students who are starting research, since it helps them survey the literature and master the relevant techniques. This method is also sometimes used by researchers to write “incremental papers.” Therefore, none of the problems in our dataset consti- tute breakthrough open problems. Solving them does not require great originality or the ability to construct new physical theories. However, it does require familiarity with current research, and the ability to understand and accurately adapt cutting-edge calculational techniques. =====PAGE 4===== 4 A. We estimate that before the advent of modern agents, only a small number of experts would have had both the physics background and familiarity with the relevant numerical techniques to solve the problem; moreover, it would have taken these experts several days to reconstruct the old calculation and perform the new one. A human expert working with a frontier AI agent can now solve this problem both accurately and rapidly with ease. Distribution of problems We obtained a total of 50 problems spanning a range of areas of theoretical physics. Table I summarizes their dis- tribution by theme. These themes cover a large number of topics. We do not believe that any physicist would have the expertise to attempt more than a fraction of these problems. Given the level of specialization in mod- ern physics, it might often be difficult for a single expert to attempt all problems in a given thematic class. Our problems do not provide an exhaustive survey of all areas of theoretical physics. The distribution of prob- lems is partly influenced by network effects, since the coordinators and moderators invited experts they knew. However, there is also an intrinsic scientific influence on the distribution. While some fields, such as quantum field theory, naturally lend themselves to a number of hard calculations with verifiable answers, it is harder to construct such problems in other fields. Evaluation As explained above, every problem in our dataset has a verifiable answer. In some cases, such as the problem given in Box 1a, our problems naturally had numerical answers that could be checked against the answer pro- vided by the agent. But it is also common to present the answers of ana- lytical calculations in physics using symbolic expressions. In some cases, it is nontrivial to verify whether a given answer matches the reference answer because the same mathematical expression can be written in many equiv- alent ways. The LLM can convert the expression provided by the agent to standardized notation and then use a symbolic manipulation program to determine its equality with a reference solution. However, this intro- duces an extraneous source of error. Therefore, we chose to convert all expressions to nu- merical values by giving the agents numerical values, both for relevant and also irrelevant physical quantities. Agent solutions were graded on these numerical an- swers. Our grading key allows for the convention choices we ob- served in our runs. Attention is also required for §1b, which, as discussed in Appendix B, is resistant to numer- ical conversion.
