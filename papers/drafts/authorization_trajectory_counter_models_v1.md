# Counter-Models for "Authorization Depends on Trajectory, Not Final State": What Each Claim Needs

**Draft v1 for review. Not peer reviewed; may be revised or withdrawn.**

**Scope of naming.** The work examined is identified by its own title and benchmark name, not by any person: the October 2026 self-published preprint *Authorization State and Trajectory in Persistent AI Agents: Testing Final-State Sufficiency* and its synthetic benchmark (AS-Bench). No author is named, no intent is attributed, and nothing here is a finding about a person. The paper examines arguments and one benchmark's headline number, not an implementation. Other works are cited by arXiv identifier and title.

**What was read.** The preprint's full text, from a saved copy of its web page (about 6,100 words of article text; no code, data, specification or reference list is linked from it, and its diagrams were read only as the text transcribed in the page). The first version of this draft was written from a screenshot and a summary note and misstated the preprint in several places; section 3a lists what changed. The author's own limits are kept throughout: the reported rates are on a synthetic benchmark, and real LLM-agent rates are not established.

**Fair-reading note.** The preprint states a deliberately narrow claim ("a controlled, reproducible experiment" distinguishing final-state sufficiency from trajectory dependence), disclaims inventing runtime authorization or authorization continuity, says its 51.1% is "not a claim that real AI agents fail authorization 51.1% of the time", and lists its own falsifiers. Much of what follows agrees with those statements; where it goes further, it says so.

**Disclosure.** Drafted with AI assistance. The defeat conditions and the toys were written by one model family. Two review passes (section 7) followed; neither was blind in the strong sense. Nothing here is independent verification.

## 1. The question

The preprint asks whether the final authorization state is sufficient to decide whether a consequential action is authorized, or whether validity can depend on the trajectory through which that state was reached. It writes the contrast as `Auth = f(A_final)` against `Auth = f(A_final, H, E)`, with H the security-relevant history and E the evidence needed to reconstruct it. Its central experiment is a matched pair: two trajectories with the same final state and different histories, where a different correct decision shows trajectory dependence. Its support is a deterministic reference experiment (fixed seed, 10,000 generated scenarios; 5,000 matched pairs) on a benchmark it names AS-Bench. Three kinds of final-state-only reference (static authorization, runtime authorization on the visible state, and runtime authorization plus provenance "that did not contain the missing transition semantics") show 48.9% accuracy and 51.1% false continuation; transition-aware references show 100% and 0%. On the matched pairs, path discrimination is 0% for final-state-only references and 100% for transition-aware ones. The preprint says these figures validate the instrument and are "the error rate of the deliberately constructed deterministic reference architecture under the benchmark's controlled semantics."

This paper asks what each claim form would have to survive. The preprint frames most of its items as hypotheses and research questions, not results; the table marks which are which.

For each claim form this paper fixes, in observable terms and before building anything, a counter-model and a defeat condition; builds a small toy; reports what happened, including the predictions that missed; and says what the claim would need to hold.

## 2. Method

- One defeat condition per claim form, **written before the toy was built** ([`PREREG.md`](authorization_trajectory_counter_models_v1/PREREG.md); later changes are dated amendments below the original text).
- Status vocabulary: **defeated at scope** (the stated observable occurred in a run), **not reached** (the toy could not produce it), **blocked** (needs an artifact not supplied), **not run**. Nothing is recorded as refuted on argument alone.
- Companion code (stdlib only, seeded, `--selftest` asserts the pinned results): [`authorization_trajectory_counter_models_v1/`](authorization_trajectory_counter_models_v1/). Counts are seed-specific; qualitative results are not.
- Several toy results are **determined by construction** (the toy encodes the mechanism). They show the structure has the stated consequence; they are not evidence that any real system has it. Each result below says which kind it is.

**What the preprint's own design already does.** (a) Its 0% path discrimination for final-state-only references on identical-final-state pairs is an identity for any function of the final state: a function of `A_final` cannot separate two inputs with equal `A_final`. The informative question is whether `A_final` can carry the history statistic, which is what T1 tests; the preprint's own list of "final-state-only" references excludes the augmented case by definition (provenance "without the missing transition semantics"). (b) It poses the sizing question itself, as future work: a minimal sufficient history `H* ⊆ H` such that `Auth(H) = Auth(H*)`. Section 5's "open question" is that question; the preprint states it first. (c) It defines a false-revalidation rate and an objective `FCR + λ·FRR`, so the cost side of CF5 is in its design, not its results (no FRR figure is reported). (d) Its limitations name an unobserved relevant transition ("a monitor that fails to observe a relevant transition cannot correctly evaluate it") and say integrity does not guarantee semantic correctness; its threat model lists monitor compromise and evidence manipulation. CF4, CF7 and CF8 are therefore acknowledged as open and untested, not missed. (e) It lists its own falsifiers, including "the effect disappears when independently generated scenarios are used"; T2's result bears on that one.

**Predictions that missed.** (1) T2: I expected the 51.1% to be reproducible near p = 1.0; the toy tops out at 0.50. (2) T3b: I expected the elapsed-plus-count baseline to be within 0.02 of the transferred score; it beat it by 0.087. The first-run reason I gave for CF3's defeat (weight overfit) was incomplete; the control shows the advantage over elapsed time is largely an unweighted change count. (3) T5b: I expected the non-atomic leak to be flat at 0.02; it falls with w because the leak counts only paths not already revoked before the read (r(1-r)^w). (4) T5b: the version-ignoring closed form I predicted was off by one tick (the gap tick is in the window).

**Harness errors kept.** T4 passed an argument it never used; T5's version-bound row was a constant; T1 rule A's "final state" included a history counter; T1 `pairs` duplicates `mism`; T5's comment said five dimensions where four were checked. All were found by the review passes in section 7 or on rereading; run 1 output is kept in `out_run1.json` and the controls are in `toys_v2.py` and `out_run2_v2controls.json`.

## 3a. Corrections made after reading the full text

The first version of this draft was written from a screenshot and a summary note. Against the full text it: (1) stated the divergence-versus-elapsed-time item as a claim, when the preprint poses it as an open research question and lists the result that would weaken it; (2) described the 51.1% as the preprint's headline, when the preprint disclaims reading it as a real-agent rate and states it is a reference-semantics figure; (3) listed false-denial cost and metric definitions as requirements the preprint lacked, when it defines false revalidation, an objective weighting it, and metric formulas (but reports no false-revalidation figure); (4) presented the sizing question for the extra statistic as this paper's open question, when the preprint states it as future work (a minimal sufficient history); (5) did not report that the preprint names three prior works and disclaims inventing runtime authorization or continuity; (6) called the preprint's four mechanisms and metrics unmeasured without noting that its limitations name the observation and integrity problems. The toy results and the prior-art findings were not affected, since the toys test structures and not the preprint's code.

## 3b. The preprint's mutation classes (T6)

After reading the full text, the toys were extended to the preprint's ten mutation classes (principal, mandate, policy, delegation, condition, resource acquisition, environment, effect escalation, evidence, instruction conflict) and its depth grid {0, 1, 3, 5, 10, 20}, with predictions fixed first (PREREG Amendment 2). This is **our own implementation of the described classes under a rule stated in the preregistration**; the preprint's code is unpublished, so none of it is the preprint's result and every class-level outcome is a property of our rule. Each class is a component with a visible bit and a hidden provenance bit; events are break, authoritative restore, silent change and ordinary; an action is authorized iff every validity component is both visible-OK and clean (the evidence class is excluded from validity).

- **Sufficient statistic (exact, by automaton minimization).** Two residual classes per validity component and 2^k composed over k components (k = 1..4: 2, 4, 8, 16): one derived bit per component, nine bits for nine validity components. Bounded, but growing with the number of components, which is the pattern the delegation-graph literature (section 6) quantifies at greater size. This is the sizing question the preprint states as future work, answered for our rule only.
- **The 51.1% is a tuning outcome.** Final-state-only false continuation reached 0.514, 0.509 and 0.534 under three different (depth, silent-change weight) settings and stayed under 0.1 at another. At depth 0, and at depth 20 with only ordinary events, it was 0: operational depth is not authority depth.
- **Prediction missed: depth does not raise false continuation.** At a fixed silent-change weight false continuation fell with depth (0.514 at depth 1 to 0.106 at depth 20 for weight 0.35) because accumulated breaks leave visible failures; error over all scenarios rose and then fell. Under this rule the preprint's question "does transition depth increase boundary errors" has no generator-independent answer.
- **Cost side.** Revalidating on every authority-relevant event gave 0.325 unnecessary revalidations over all transitions at the stated mix; a class-aware policy 0 (by construction).
- **Equal-final-state pairs.** In 60,000 generated scenarios, 43.3% of equal-final-state pairs differed in ground truth; the 0% and 100% path-discrimination figures are identities and are not measured.
- **Independent re-implementation of T6.** A fresh instance of the same model family implemented T6 from a written specification only (no code, no results), after filing its own predictions. Part A matched exactly (2, 4, 8, 16 classes). False continuation for the final-state-only reference matched within sampling error (for example 0.512 vs 0.514 at weight 0.35, depth 1; 0.533 vs 0.534 at weight 0.8, depth 20), and the equal-final-state differing-truth fraction was 0.435 vs 0.433. This checks the arithmetic and the specification's completeness, not the rule; same family. It resolved three specification ambiguities by choice (component order, a nan rather than 0 for zero-opportunity cells, and grouping that includes the evidence bit), none of which moved a result.

## 3c. CF7 to CF10 (T7 to T10)

Predictions were fixed first (PREREG Amendment 3). The component rule and generator are T6's (depth 10, silent weight 0.35).
- **Reconstructability (CF7).** With a complete authoritative log the transition-aware reference has false continuation 0 and false revalidation 0. With the log missing, default-allow gives the final-state-only rate (0.232); default-deny gives 0 and denies every authorized scenario (stated, not simulated). With an agent-writable log where the agent drops each adverse event with probability q: false continuation 0, 0.059, 0.197, 0.465, 1.0 at q = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0. **Missed:** I predicted 0.4 to 0.8 at q = 0.5; the curve is convex because a scenario stays unauthorized if any adverse event survives. With two logs, "any adverse wins" gave 0 and "agent log wins" gave 1.0 (by construction): the resolution rule decides the result.
- **Replay (CF8).** A replayed old restore event appended after a later break (probability 0.3): false continuation 0.003 without a per-component sequence check and 0 with one. **Missed:** I predicted above 0.05; a replay changes the end state only when it repairs the single remaining bad component, which is rare under this generator, so the exposure is small here and generator-dependent.
- **Queued effects (CF9).** One check before a queue of 10 effects spaced 5 ticks apart, revocation rate 0.02 per tick: the share executed after revocation rises with queue position from 0.02 to 0.60 (mean 0.348). A non-atomic per-effect check leaks 0.02 per effect; atomic compare-and-consume gives 0 (stated). By construction.
- **Redundancy and delegation composition (CF10).** Pairs equal in the augmented state that differ in ground truth: 0 (an identity: once the maintained state carries the derived bit, history adds nothing for this rule; the preprint's maintained-state design and the augmentation are the same thing). A delegation chain where each hop silently adds an out-of-ceiling effect with probability 0.1 violates the subset invariant at 0.097, 0.269, 0.411, 0.65 for 1, 3, 5, 10 hops; with enforced attenuation 0 (by construction).
- **Real traces (CF11)** are not a toy; blocked.

## 4. Claim forms added after review (results for CF7 to CF10 in section 3c), defeat conditions fixed, not run

- **CF7 reconstructability (E).** Defeat: with H present but E missing, delayed, contradictory or agent-writable, a transition-aware reference's false-continuation rate rises to the final-state reference's rate.
- **CF8 state-source integrity.** Delay is covered by T4b. Defeat for replay and freshness: an independent but replayed or stale event stream produces a wrong decision.
- **CF9 atomicity and concurrency.** T5b covers one effect. Defeat for concurrent queued effects: check, bind and commit not atomic against revocation or delegation changes leaves post-revocation effects.
- **CF10 redundancy.** If a trusted transition function maintains the state, history is already folded into it and `f(A_final, H, E)` and `f(A_final)` coincide. Defeat: a rule where the maintained state decides every matched pair correctly.
- **CF11 real traces.** The preprint states real LLM-agent rates are not established and names real-agent runs as the next stage; prior work with real traces exists (section 6).

## 5. What the defeats share

CF1, CF2 and CF3 share one structure: **the result is determined by what the instrument is allowed to see, and the instrument was built by the claimant.** A reference defined as blind to H, scored on labels derived from H, fails where the labels need H; a divergence score fitted to a label rule predicts that rule. CF4 and CF5 are the channel-collapse and check-to-use structures from [`pre_execution_gate_counter_models_v1.md`](pre_execution_gate_counter_models_v1.md), at another site; their exits are the same (an independent channel, deny on silence, an effect bound to the checked version with atomic compare and act). The preprint's separation of a trusted transition function from the proposing model is the right instinct; it is also what makes the augmentation counter-model easy, since a state holding principal, mandate, delegation, effect ceiling and validity with a trusted transition function is already a final state that includes history statistics. The open question is how large the extra statistic must be.

## 6. Prior work

Nine arXiv preprints were read in part (abstract, introduction, grep hits and cited sections; appendices mostly not):
- **2609.08062v2** (30 Sep 2026), *ResidualAuth: What Authorization State Must Agent Systems Preserve under Revocable Delegation?* Read in full by a delegated reader against the text. Its first-page abstract states the matched-pair formulation (identical current permissions, opposite decisions after the same revocation). Its formal core: an exact deterministic online monitor needs exactly as many states as the number of residual classes (a standard Myhill–Nerode instantiation; the paper says its contribution "is not a new automata theorem", in an appendix); for N ≥ 2 there are families with identical all-pairs closure and at least 2^(N(N−1)/2) residual states (a lower bound, not an exact count); exact counts for three delegation semantics; and an average-error lower bound under an explicit information limit. Its benchmark has 192 matched pairs (16 per setting in the published split) scored pair-complete with a symbolic grader. Empirical results are from four open-weight models and one hosted model on fixed constructions, with null results stated (no strict-memory contrast survives multiplicity correction; the "1/128" figure is one strict-memory pair for one model in an appendix table; a trusted supplied decision gives 15 to 16 of 16 pairs against 0 to 2 of 16 from a 256-token summary). It states its bounds concern exact monitoring, not language-model context length, assumes authenticated principals and events, and treats stale-state safety as unvalidated. No freshness or divergence score, no elapsed-time baseline, no replay or atomicity analysis beyond a commit-time hard gate, and no compression algorithm for a minimal history (its "full residual state" serialization is sufficient but not necessarily minimal). My T1 uses the same method at toy scale and is not independent of it.
- **2609.01836v1** (1 Sep 2026), *Agent Memory Is a Surface for Endogenous Authorization Laundering.* Read in full by a delegated reader. LLM memory writers lose or distort authorization state; a hidden deterministic ledger scores matched pairs; 5 writers, 2 executors, 3 seeds, 3 domains on LLM-generated, manually reviewed histories. The 50.2% false-authority figure is typed memory with incremental updates in the finance domain only (the worst domain; procurement 28.3%, cybersecurity 10.4%; pooled request-level 24.9%). Executors acted on false authority in 205 of 208 trials (98.6%), a selected set. The source-event gate, which uses gold knowledge of who may grant, cut unauthorized submission from 25.3% to 7.3% while authorized use fell from 93.3% to 53.8%; bounded event sourcing cut it to 9.0% with authorized use 64.7% (13.3% in finance). Stated limits: more structured than real communication, no prevalence estimate, per-writer results on one seed, mitigations assume known principals. Atomic check-and-effect and concurrency are out of scope; "replay" means the ledger function, not replay attacks.
- **2609.33910v1** (27 Sep 2026), *When Consent Outlives Context: Residual Authority Replay in Long-Lived Agents.* Read in full by a delegated reader. Eight production coding agents were profiled for how approvals persist (persistence varies by agent; three were run live). In an emulated authorization layer over 508 AgentDojo cases, the maximum attack-success gain over a fresh state is 35.1 percentage points (one model, history length 64, tool-level matching); two of six model families show about 0 to 0.4, fresh success is 0 by construction, and exact-resource matching shrinks the effect to 0.012 to 0.039. The live +24.9 percentage points is a case-weighted mean of three agent-level means over 55 context-rebinding cases (not prompt injection), five repetitions, some classes of two cases, no interval reported. Qualitative statement that the same action can execute without renewed approval under one history and require it under another; no formal information-limit result; time-of-check analogy only; revalidation proposed as future design; event-source integrity assumed.
- **2609.02866v1** (2 Sep 2026), *When Does Authorization End? Effect Closure at Provider Boundaries.* Version-binding and revocation-to-effect gaps in GitHub, Kubernetes, NATS and Kafka.
- **2608.21159v1**, *AID-Guard: Stateful Authorization for Delegated Agent Effects.* Revalidation at commit; a strict exact-manifest profile reduced benign utility by 35.4 to 43.8 percentage points.
- **2609.21284v1**, *Authorization Revocation for Long-Running AI Agents: Root-Scoped Quiescence under Delegation and Asynchronous Execution.* Epoch fences and certificates.
- **2609.31301v1**, *Beyond Approved Actions: Runtime Validation of Persistent Outcomes in Agent Workflows.* Effect comparison against what was approved for the current state.
- **2609.28586v1**, *Agent Approval Laundering: Transitive Effects Beyond the Approved Invocation.* Read in full by a delegated reader. Its formal core is a short pigeonhole-style lemma: if two execution instances have identical policy-visible fields and different required effect-specific decisions, no record-only policy, deterministic or randomized, can be right on both; it is conditional on such pairs existing, and the empirical evidence is coverage gaps, not measured indistinguishable decision-differing pairs. The 111 records are deduplicated successful records; the 40 to 17 to 13 residual-record ladder's last step is post hoc with a parser partly developed on this corpus, and all 13 are in a harness fixture stratum. Held-out predictor: 17 of 18 prespecified workflows reached execution, macro recall 0.926 and precision 0.941 against 0.770 and 0.941 for a command-only baseline, with fixed-cohort stability intervals, not population intervals; development cohorts scored lower (0.694 and 0.732 on one). The hook demonstration is one request. Paper-only release; no atomic enforcement evaluated; temporal binding is a rule (decision inputs frozen before execution), not tested as a check-to-use gap.
- **2609.15906v1**, *Authorization Architectures for Tool-Using AI Agents* (a review). Names task-aware revalidation as an unresolved requirement.

A string search of all nine for the preprint's score names ("decision freshness", "authority-state divergence", "survivab*") found none; the transition classes were not searched. This does not show the preprint's own elements are absent elsewhere. Whether the preprint cites any of these is unknown.

## 8. What a trajectory-dependent authorization claim needs

1. A stated kind of bound on the extra statistic, and an exhibited rule for which no bounded augmentation reproduces the history-aware answer, or an argument that the engineering barrier is real (CF1).
2. Benchmark labels from an independent source, the label-from-history fraction reported, and real agent traces before any sentence about rates (CF2, CF11).
3. Weights and distance fixed before evaluation, an unweighted change-count baseline and an elapsed-plus-count baseline, and a held-out generator that does not share a common cause (CF3).
4. The producer of events and evidence named and independent of the agent, a default of deny on silence, a delivery bound, and replay and freshness protection (CF4, CF7, CF8).
5. The effect bound to the checked version with an atomic compare-and-act and an effector that honors it (CF5, CF9).
6. A false-denial (false-revalidation) rate reported beside every false-continuation rate, with "benign" defined without presupposing the relevant dimension (CF5). The preprint defines both rates and an objective weighting them; it reports only the false-continuation figure.
7. Operational definitions for each named property. The preprint gives metric formulas (survivability rate as correct decisions over consequential decisions, boundary detection accuracy, revalidation accuracy, evidence completeness, path-dependence effect); the survivability rate is accuracy, so its four named dimensions (applicability, boundedness, revalidation, reconstructability) are not separately scored in what was read.
8. The prior work in section 6 positioned.

## 9. What this does not establish

The reference implementation, scenario generator and seed outputs are not published in the page that was read, so nothing here touches the preprint's own numbers: its 51.1% is neither reproduced nor refuted, and the toys test structures, not its code. The preprint's diagrams were read only as transcribed text. Items the preprint specifies but this paper did not test: the mutation classes and depth grid, the ablation list, the four-way risk-by-authorization design, evidence debt, and the effect-ceiling and delegation-subset invariants. Toys T2, T4 and the atomic row of T5 are determined by construction. T1's class counts are lower bounds (suffix length equals prefix length), each toy ran with one seed, and the toy rules are far simpler than a delegation graph, where the cited work shows the required statistic can be exponential in delegation redundancy. Both review passes are same-source in the sense that the brief and code came from one family. The claim that trajectory matters to authorization in some rules is plausible and is not what is tested; what is tested is whether the headline and the "final-state insufficiency" framing survive the representation and circularity attacks. A narrower reading survives: trajectory information can matter, history is not shown to be irreducible, benchmark error rates can be artifacts of label prevalence, learned divergence metrics can fail under generator shift, a trusted transition function needs an independent event channel, and revalidation alone does not close a check-to-use race.

## What would show this paper wrong

A reader who supplies the benchmark's labels and finds they come from an independent source and survive a hand-set-p check; a rule shown to have an unbounded statistic that engineering cannot bound; the preprint's divergence score, fixed in advance, beating an unweighted change count on a generator it was not tuned on; or a published statement of the preprint's score or transition classes that predates it.
