# Real-Time Specimen Analysis: The Dead-Salmon fMRI Post — Factual Drift, and Ring 3 Demonstrating Itself in the Comments

### A LinkedIn retelling of a famous statistics cautionary tale softens and misstates the original result, and the comment section — while explicitly praising the post's lesson about unchecked statistical claims — repeats the misstated number forward without checking it

---

## Executive Summary

A LinkedIn post by Yukti Chopra retells the well-known "dead salmon fMRI" study (Bennett et al., 2009) as a lesson about multiple-comparisons correction. Checked directly against the actual study: the post softens the experimental task, misstates a key number (claiming "around 130,000 voxels" where the actual paper's search volume was 8,064), and omits the single most extraordinary, independently-verifiable fact of the real story — that the field's rate of uncorrected multiple-comparisons analyses measurably dropped, from 25–40% of published fMRI papers in 2009 to roughly 10% by 2012, a change the Ig Nobel committee explicitly credited this work with helping produce. The comment thread beneath the post is the specimen's second layer: multiple credentialed commenters extend the post's lesson correctly in the abstract (Type I error inflation, AI pattern-finding bias), while one commenter — a lab founder — repeats the inaccurate "130,000 voxels" figure verbatim as the explanatory mechanism, and no one in the visible thread checks it. The thread demonstrates, on itself, in real time, the exact failure mode it is collectively praising the post for exposing.

---

## The Specimen: What Was Posted, Checked Against the Original Study

**The post (Yukti Chopra, LinkedIn, "Understanding the brain through Computational Cogni[tive Neuroscience]," posted 4 days prior).** Summarized: a "post-mortem Atlantic salmon" was placed in an fMRI scanner and "shown photographs of people in different social situations"; analysis without correcting for multiple comparisons found "apparently significant activity"; "an fMRI scan contains around 130,000 voxels," making spurious findings likely; after "proper corrections," the activity "disappeared"; closes with "Sometimes the best way to reveal a problem in neuroscience is to put a dead fish in a brain scanner" and a link captioned "the legendary research paper."

**Checked against Bennett, Baird, Miller & Wolford, "Neural correlates of interspecies perspective taking in the post-mortem Atlantic Salmon: An argument for multiple comparisons correction" (presented at the 2009 Organization for Human Brain Mapping conference; 2012 Ig Nobel Prize in Neuroscience):**

1. **The task is softened.** The salmon was not merely shown photos — it was shown photographs of humans in social situations and, per the study's own design, asked to judge what emotion the pictured individual was experiencing: a perspective-taking / mentalizing task, which is what the paper's title ("interspecies perspective taking") actually names, and which is the specific mechanism making the result absurd — a dead fish cannot perform perspective-taking, so any "significant" activation is transparently spurious by construction. The post's vaguer phrasing loses the part that makes the joke work.
2. **The voxel count does not match the study.** Checked directly: the paper's search volume was **8,064 voxels**, of which 16 were significant before correction, and none survived FDR or FWER correction even at a relaxed threshold (p = 0.25). "Around 130,000 voxels" is not this study's figure. It is roughly plausible as a generic whole-brain fMRI acquisition size in other contexts, but presented here as the explanation for *this* result, it misstates the actual numbers Bennett et al. reported.
3. **The most extraordinary, checkable fact of the real story is omitted entirely.** When first presented in 2009, an estimated 25–40% of published fMRI papers reported no correction for multiple comparisons. By 2012, when the work won the Ig Nobel Prize, that figure had dropped to roughly 10% — a real, measured, field-wide change in methodological practice, credited in part to this demonstration. The post replaces this with generic, unfalsifiable closing commentary ("good statistics help us distinguish genuine discoveries from coincidences") and never mentions the Ig Nobel Prize. The part of the story with an actual dated, measurable outcome is the part that didn't survive the retelling.

## The Comment Thread: Ring 3 Demonstrating Itself

**`mirror_test_v1.md` names the mechanism already; this thread is a live instance of it.** §5.1's "Ring 3 — Academic legitimacy systems" states: "Peer review, citation networks, conference program committees, and hiring pipelines... do not merely validate findings; they validate the *frame* within which findings are assessed." What follows is that mechanism observed directly in a comment section, not inferred from theory.

**The correct extensions.** M.N. Gupta (PhD, FNASc, FNA, Former Professor) draws a reasonable analogy to AI systems finding spurious patterns and exhibiting "bias... towards agreeing to a given hypothesis." Charles Kraemer states the underlying statistics correctly: "as you increase the number of analyses on the same set of data, you artificially increase alpha, the probability of a Type I error." Adam Weiss (AI | BCI | Neuro | Systems) adds a generically correct point about scaling and statistical testing. None of these is wrong in the abstract, and none of them touches the post's specific numbers.

**The specific propagation, checked and confirmed.** Vidhi Modi (Founder, VedLabs | Brain Science Lab) writes: *"A dead salmon 'having brain activity' is such a good gotcha moment... 130,000 voxels means you'll find 'something' if you don't correct for it, that's the whole trap. Best reminder that good stats matter more than a good scan."* This restates the post's inaccurate figure as the operative explanatory mechanism, delivered with the added authority of a stated lab-founder credential, to two more likes of agreement. Nothing in the visible thread checks the number against the source paper (8,064, not 130,000).

**The pattern named precisely.** Every visible commenter is applying real statistical literacy to the post's *lesson* while extending zero scrutiny to the post's *numbers*. The thread's social reward structure — likes, agreement, competent elaboration — reinforces fluency and consensus, not verification. This is the specific, observable content of "validating the frame, not the finding": the frame ("unchecked statistics produce false patterns") is correct and gets validated repeatedly; the finding (this specific number, in this specific post) gets neither checked nor corrected, and is actively amplified by someone credentialed enough that a reader would reasonably expect them to know better.

## What This Case Study Does Not Claim

Does not assert any individual commenter acted in bad faith — the more likely and more interesting reading is that competent people extending a correct general principle simply did not think to fact-check a specific number embedded in someone else's post, which is itself the point: verification failure here required no dishonesty from anyone, only the ordinary social physics of a comment thread. Does not claim the underlying Bennett et al. study or its lesson about multiple-comparisons correction is wrong — it is real, well-documented, and the post's central lesson is directionally correct; what drifted is the specific supporting detail, not the conclusion. Does not have visibility into the full comment thread (the screenshot is a mid-thread view with a "Collapse replies" marker above it and at least one comment, from Mark Stecker, cut off before its content was visible) — this case study describes only what was directly inspected, not the thread's full extent.
